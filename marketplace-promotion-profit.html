Ask what the sale leaves behind.

The promotion is working. Orders are coming in, the sales chart is climbing, and the team is discussing how long to extend the offer. Then someone asks a harder question: how much money is each additional order leaving behind?

A successful marketplace operation needs an answer before the offer scales. A lower price may increase demand while leaving too little room for product cost, selling fees, delivery, advertising, and returns. That gap can stay hidden when the weekly review stops at revenue.

My preferred starting point is one practical question: what contribution does this SKU generate on this channel, under this offer? That makes the discussion specific enough to support a pricing, replenishment, or campaign decision.

Define the cost boundary first.

For an operational promotion review, define contribution as customer revenue after discounts, excluding collected sales tax, less the product cost and the variable costs included in the model. Include customer-paid shipping if it is revenue in your comparison, and include the corresponding delivery expense consistently.

A useful working definition

Contribution = revenue after discounts − product cost − selling and payment fees − fulfillment costs − allocated advertising − expected return losses.

This is a planning measure before fixed overhead. It is not a claim about accounting net profit. Document exactly what each line includes.

Separate shipping the product into your network from shipping it to the customer. Record where inbound freight, packaging, preparation, and other incremental costs sit so they are counted once. For a multi-channel review, use a consistent definition while retaining the costs specific to each channel.

Amazon provides tools to estimate product fees and compare fulfillment methods. Use those as planning inputs, then reconcile them with the costs actually charged. [1] Category-specific fees should come from the applicable fee schedule; one assumed percentage is not a universal Amazon rate. [2]

A 10% discount can remove more than 10% of the contribution.

Consider the following hypothetical product. All values are illustrative assumptions, not results from my employer, an account I manage, or a marketplace fee quotation.

Illustrative economics per original order, in USD
ItemRegular offer10% discount
Selling price after discount$100.00$90.00
Product cost−$60.00−$60.00
Assumed selling fee: 15%−$15.00−$13.50
Fulfillment and packaging−$6.00−$6.00
Allocated advertising−$8.00−$8.00
Expected return loss allowance−$3.00−$3.00
Contribution before fixed overhead$8.00−$0.50

The discount reduces revenue by $10 and saves $1.50 in the assumed percentage fee. Contribution therefore falls by $8.50. If the discounted offer generated 150 orders instead of 100, revenue would rise from $10,000 to $13,500 while modeled contribution would fall from $800 to −$75. The extra volume is an assumption here, not a forecast of how customers would respond.

The example holds other costs constant to isolate the price change. In practice, advertising cost per order, fulfillment expense, and return behavior can also change. Test those separately instead of treating the first calculation as a complete forecast.

The return allowance represents the expected economic loss from future returns, including revenue reversals, relevant costs, and inventory or fee recoveries. The selling-price line has not already deducted those future refunds. Once a cohort matures, replace the allowance with observed outcomes; subtracting both would double-count the loss.

Build a SKU view you can trust.

Start with a small, consistent set of fields: order date, channel, order-line identifier, SKU, quantity, realized price, cost effective at the time of sale, fees, fulfillment expense, and return status. Keep refunds and adjustments linked to the original order wherever the source data allows.

Shopify’s gross-profit reports depend on cost information being recorded when a product or variant is sold. Its documentation also explains that discounts and refunds affect reported margins. [3] Updating a product’s cost later does not rewrite the cost attached to earlier orders. [4] A blank cost field therefore needs investigation; treating it as zero can make an incomplete record look highly profitable.

In SQL, aggregate each source to a compatible level before joining it. Fees may arrive as several events against one order. Advertising may exist only at campaign level. Keep the allocation rule visible rather than presenting campaign-level spending as an exact order-level fact.

In Power BI, I would separate observed costs from estimates and show the age of each order cohort. Compare contribution per order, total contribution, and the share of orders with complete cost data. A margin figure accompanied by a coverage measure is easier to evaluate than a precise-looking percentage with missing inputs.

Set guardrails before launching the offer.

A promotion should have a stated purpose: profitable volume, inventory clearance, or a controlled acquisition experiment. The decision criteria can differ, but the expected cost should be visible in each case.

For the simplified example above, the costs outside the 15% fee total $77. The contribution break-even price is $77 ÷ 0.85, or approximately $90.59 before fixed overhead. To retain an $8 contribution under the same assumptions, the price would need to be ($77 + $8) ÷ 0.85 = $100.

This calculation is a sensitivity check, not a universal pricing formula. Tiered fees, minimum charges, changing advertising costs, shipping revenue, taxes, and return recovery can require a different model. Use the actual fee basis and cost structure for the product being reviewed.

Agree on the minimum acceptable contribution, maximum experiment cost, review date, and owner before launch. Then examine a higher-return or higher-advertising-cost scenario. A promotion that only works under the most optimistic assumptions needs a smaller test or a different offer.

Make the next action specific.

Begin with the products that sell the most and the products whose contribution has fallen the most. For each exception, identify whether the change comes from price, product cost, fees, fulfillment, advertising, or returns. Comparing the same SKU and channel across equivalent periods helps separate a real cost change from a shift in the product mix.

A practical promotion review
  1. Confirm the revenue and cost definitions.
  2. Reconcile fees and flag missing cost records.
  3. Compare regular and promotional unit economics.
  4. Test advertising and return-loss assumptions.
  5. Assign an owner and a date for the pricing or operational change.

The response may be a revised price, a different bundle, a packaging correction, a narrower campaign, or a deliberate clearance decision. Record why it was chosen and what outcome would justify continuing.

Growth becomes easier to manage when the team can explain what it costs to earn each sale. That is the connection I want between marketplace operations and analytics: a reliable number that leads to a clear decision.
