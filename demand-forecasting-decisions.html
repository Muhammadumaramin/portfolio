A useful forecast prepares you for more than one outcome.

A practical approach to e-commerce forecasting: establish a benchmark, test honestly, explain uncertainty, and connect the forecast to replenishment decisions.

The central idea

Judge a forecast by how honestly it represents uncertainty and how well it supports the decision you need to make.

Define the decision and horizon first.

“Predict next month’s sales” sounds specific until the replenishment team asks which product, which channel, and when an order must be placed. A useful forecasting brief starts with the decision: buying stock, scheduling labor, or planning cash. Those decisions require different time horizons and levels of detail.

For replenishment, write down the review frequency, supplier lead time, and the inventory position available at the decision date. A weekly product forecast may be more useful than a precise-looking daily total for the entire store. Do not spend weeks improving a model before confirming that its output fits the operating process.

My proposed brief has five fields: target, granularity, forecast horizon, information available at the cutoff, and the action the forecast will support. Add the cost of getting the decision wrong in each direction. Too much stock and too little stock are not interchangeable business outcomes.

Earn complexity by beating a simple benchmark.

Begin with a transparent baseline. The forecasting textbook Forecasting: Principles and Practice describes naïve and seasonal-naïve methods: the latter uses the last observed value from the corresponding season. It also recommends evaluating forecasts against data not used to fit the model. [1]

Choose a seasonal pattern that the history can support. A new product may not have a full annual cycle; a changing catalog may make last year’s comparison misleading. Keep those limitations visible. A complicated method is not automatically useful merely because it produces a smoother chart.

Maintain a model comparison sheet with the baseline, candidate method, evaluation periods, forecast horizon, and business interpretation. Include maintenance effort: a small improvement that depends on unreliable external inputs may be less useful than a stable, understandable process.

Make the test resemble the real decision.

Time-series evaluation should respect chronology. Rolling-origin tests repeatedly train on earlier observations and evaluate later ones. Percentage errors can become undefined or unstable when actual values are zero or close to zero, so metric choice matters. Prediction intervals express uncertainty around future observations rather than certainty about one number. These principles are explained in the textbook’s forecasting toolbox. [1]

In a practical implementation, save each forecast with its issue date and target date. That lets you compare what the team actually knew against what happened, rather than comparing a revised model with history it has already seen.

Check the availability date of every input. A promotion planned at the cutoff is different from the final discount recorded afterward. Similarly, a later stock adjustment cannot quietly become an input to an earlier purchasing decision. These checks belong in the data preparation process, not just the final presentation.

Investigate what the sales history leaves out.

Recorded sales are constrained by the ability to sell. A product that was unavailable may show low sales even when customers wanted it. In an operational review, annotate unavailable periods, listing suspensions, major promotions, and product replacements before interpreting the pattern.

Do not fill every unavailable day with an invented demand figure and then treat it as an observation. Keep estimated values separate, record the method, and compare results with and without the adjustment. Where the evidence is weak, explain the uncertainty instead of hiding it inside the training data.

New products also need a different discussion. An analogous product can provide a planning starting point, but the analogy should name the similarities and differences. Price, channel, customer base, and launch support may all limit how much history transfers.

Translate the forecast into a conditional plan.

Hypothetical planning example

Suppose expected demand over a review period plus lead time is 120 units, inventory position is 90 units, and the team has separately chosen a 20-unit buffer. A simplified order-up-to calculation suggests 120 + 20 − 90 = 50 units.

This illustrates arithmetic only. The buffer is an assumption, not a model-derived service guarantee. Supplier minimums, cash, shelf life, lead-time uncertainty, and pack sizes can change the actual order.

Present alternatives with clear labels. A downside or upside business scenario is a conditional assumption; it should not be labeled a statistical interval unless it was estimated and validated as one. Explain what action changes when demand or lead time differs from the central plan.

Close the review by assigning a forecast owner and a decision owner. Save overrides with their reasons and review whether they helped. A disciplined forecasting process makes assumptions visible, learns from errors, and gives the operation enough time to respond. That is more valuable than a precise number with no explanation of what to do next.
