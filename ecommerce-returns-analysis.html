Returns are a feedback loop, not just a refund.

How to connect return reasons, order cohorts, product quality, and recovery costs to practical improvements in an e-commerce operation.

The central idea

A useful returns review connects the original purchase to a verified reason, a financial consequence, and an operational improvement.

Separate the events before calculating the rate.

A refund, a return request, a received parcel, and a restocked item are different events. Combining them into one “returns” column makes it difficult to understand either customer experience or inventory. A customer may receive a partial refund without shipping anything back; a returned item may arrive but be unsuitable for resale.

Shopify’s sales reporting documentation distinguishes sales reversals and reversed quantities from physical product returns. Reversals can include cancellations and order edits, so a negative sales adjustment is not sufficient evidence that a product came back. Check the current field definition before labeling a chart. [1]

For an internal model, preserve the order-line identifier, request date, receipt date, refund event, received quantity, inspection outcome, and disposition. Keep events at their natural level of detail before summarizing them. Otherwise, joining several refunds to several return events can accidentally multiply amounts.

Compare orders that have had time to return.

Dividing this week’s received returns by this week’s new orders mixes two different groups of customers. That ratio can still describe workload relative to current sales, but it should not be presented as the probability that this week’s orders will return.

My suggested customer-outcome view groups orders by their purchase or delivery period, then follows each group through a clearly stated observation window. Define which orders qualify, how partial returns count, and whether the window begins at purchase or delivery. Compare groups only when they have had similar time to mature.

Hypothetical example

A cohort contains 1,000 delivered units. After the chosen observation window, 80 units have physically returned: 80 ÷ 1,000 = 8%.

This is a custom delivered-unit cohort measure, not a platform’s default metric. A newer cohort with only two weeks of observation is not directly comparable to one observed for two months.

Treat the selected reason as a starting hypothesis.

“Not as described” can point to unclear copy, a wrong variant, a packing error, or a product defect. The customer’s selection is valuable evidence, but it is not a complete root-cause investigation. Preserve both the customer-reported reason and the inspection finding instead of overwriting one with the other.

Use a short, stable reason taxonomy with an “unknown” category. Ask reviewers to describe the evidence behind a recategorization. Audit a small sample of records regularly to see whether different people classify the same situation consistently.

Compare reason patterns by SKU, supplier lot, channel, and fulfillment route where the data supports it. Avoid publishing customer details in a shared dashboard. For very small groups, show the count and uncertainty rather than declaring a meaningful trend from one or two events.

Follow the item and the money separately.

A refund amount is not the full operational cost of a return, and it should not simply be added again if the revenue measure already includes that refund. Create a reconciliation that distinguishes revenue adjustments from incremental handling costs and inventory value changes.

  • Record return shipping and processing costs where available.
  • Track inspection and repackaging work using an agreed costing method.
  • Distinguish resale-ready stock from repair, liquidation, and disposal.
  • Document the basis for any inventory write-down or recovery estimate.

Use consistent accounting definitions with the finance team before presenting a profit impact. Operationally, monitor time from receipt to disposition: a returned unit that sits uninspected cannot confidently be promised to the next customer.

Turn the dashboard into a weekly improvement queue.

In SQL, build separate order-line and return-event tables, aggregate each to the intended reporting level, and reconcile totals before joining them. In Power BI, provide a cohort view for customer outcomes and a received-date view for warehouse workload. Label them clearly so users understand why the totals differ.

Select a few issues with enough evidence to act. Repeated compatibility questions may justify a listing clarification. A verified packaging problem may require a packing change. An unexplained spike should trigger investigation before anyone attributes it to a supplier or employee.

Give each action an owner, a completion date, and a follow-up measure. Compare mature cohorts after the change, while noting any concurrent promotions or product changes. The goal is to reduce avoidable disappointment and recover stock responsibly—not to make the chart look better by changing reason codes or discouraging legitimate returns.
