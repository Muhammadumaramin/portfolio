Your dashboard should end with a decision.

How to connect sales, margin, inventory, and customer experience in a weekly e-commerce review—without mistaking more charts for better analysis.

Choose the decision before choosing the chart.

A dashboard can show record sales and still leave a team unsure what to do. Perhaps sales rose because prices fell, a low-margin product dominated the mix, or delayed orders finally cleared. The headline number is a starting point, not a conclusion.

Begin the weekly review with a decision the team can make. Should a promotion continue? Which products need replenishment? Where is a fulfillment delay becoming a customer problem? Each question needs a different level of detail. A channel summary may reveal the issue, while a SKU-level view helps someone resolve it.

For each dashboard section, write one sentence: “We use this view to decide ___.” If nobody can complete the sentence, reconsider the chart. This is my proposed reporting approach, not a platform requirement or a claim that every business needs the same dashboard.

Create a metric dictionary people can actually use.

Before comparing channels, agree on what the numbers mean. Shopify’s field reference, for example, distinguishes gross sales, net sales, and total sales, and defines average order value separately from post-order adjustments. [1] A column named “sales” is therefore not enough to establish comparability.

A practical metric dictionary records the business meaning, formula, source, grain, exclusions, timezone, refresh schedule, and responsible owner. State whether order counts exclude cancellations and test orders. Document whether shipping, tax, discounts, refunds, and fees are included. Keep the definitions close to the report so reviewers do not have to rely on memory.

Conversion deserves particular care. An order-based rate and a converted-session rate are not interchangeable. Use the platform’s documented measure for within-channel analysis, and explain limitations before comparing it with another channel. If session data is unavailable or differently defined, do not invent a unified conversion percentage.

Build the model around the grain of the data.

In a SQL or Power BI workflow, the grain describes what one row represents. An order-line table might contain one row per product in an order; a payment table may contain several transactions for that order. Joining them directly can multiply values and make an apparently polished dashboard wrong.

Consider an illustrative order with three product lines and two payment events. A naive join on the order identifier can produce six rows. If the order total is repeated on each row, summing it will overstate revenue. Resolve the model before adjusting the visualization: aggregate to a compatible grain, allocate shared amounts using a documented rule, or keep separate facts linked through appropriate dimensions.

Check row counts, unique keys, and reconciliation totals before and after transformations. A refund may occur weeks after the original order; an inventory snapshot describes a point in time rather than a sale. Treat these as distinct events. The objective is not one giant table but a model whose relationships preserve the business meaning.

Read revenue alongside the cost of earning it.

A sales increase can hide weaker unit economics. Define a contribution measure that fits your operating decisions, separating it from accounting profit. For this example, contribution means net revenue less product cost and specified variable selling and fulfillment costs. Fixed overhead is excluded, and the costs included must remain consistent across comparisons.

Illustrative promotion review

Before a promotion: 100 orders × $100 net revenue = $10,000. At $75 variable cost per order, contribution is $2,500. During the promotion: 140 orders × $90 = $12,600. At the same $75 variable cost per order, contribution is $2,100.

Orders rose 40% and revenue rose 26%, but contribution fell 16%. These are hypothetical values, not results from my employer or a portfolio project. Real analysis must also account for cost changes and returns that have not yet matured.

This does not automatically mean the promotion should stop. Customer acquisition, stock clearance, or future purchases might be explicit objectives. It means the team should evaluate the promotion against its intended purpose and the costs of achieving it, rather than treating higher revenue as a complete answer.

Separate a signal from a convenient explanation.

When a metric changes, examine the comparison period and product mix before telling a story. Compare equivalent weekdays, check whether a promotion moved between dates, and identify products that were unavailable. A seasonal business can look weak against an unusually strong week even when the underlying operation is stable.

Break the change into manageable pieces: channel, product, price, quantity, and customer or order segment where appropriate. Use a consistent set of products when examining like-for-like performance, and show new or discontinued products separately. A blended average can improve simply because the mix changed.

Keep a change log for pricing, advertising, listings, stock recovery, and operational incidents. A dashboard can identify an association, but it cannot by itself prove causation. For a claim that a listing change increased sales, use a suitable experiment where available or explain the limitations of the comparison. If an AI assistant generates a summary, check its figures and supporting filters before using that narrative in a business review.

Make the weekly review produce a small action list.

A useful review has three outputs: the change that matters, the best-supported explanation, and the next action. Start with a small number of exceptions rather than reading every chart aloud. Show the period, refresh time, and any missing data before interpreting the results.

  • Commercial decision: continue, revise, or stop an offer based on its agreed objective.
  • Operational decision: investigate a stock mismatch, revise a handling promise, or resolve a recurring order exception.
  • Data decision: repair a definition, reconciliation, or missing source before drawing a conclusion.

For each action, record one owner, a due date, the evidence that triggered it, and how completion will be assessed. Revisit the previous action list at the next review. Otherwise, the dashboard risks becoming a recurring presentation of the same unresolved problems.

My actual-versus-target sales dashboard project shows a performance-comparison approach. Adapting it to a business requires that business’s definitions, costs, data access, and decisions. A dashboard is valuable when it helps someone ask a better question and follow through—not merely when every indicator turns green.
