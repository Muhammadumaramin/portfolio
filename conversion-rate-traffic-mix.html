Conversion improved. Why did the overall rate fall?

A worked e-commerce example of traffic mix, weighted conversion rates, and a Power BI measure that helps separate segment performance from audience changes.

Before changing the campaign, inspect the denominator.

The weekly report is uncomfortable: the overall conversion rate has fallen, yet every traffic segment on the supporting page shows an improvement. Is the report broken, or did the business get worse? Neither conclusion follows automatically. A change in the composition of traffic can reverse the direction of the aggregate.

This matters during promotions, when a campaign can bring a different audience to the store. A headline percentage may trigger a discount, a landing-page redesign, or a budget cut before anyone checks which sessions now make up that percentage. The immediate analytical task is to separate changing segment rates from changing segment weights.

The method below is a diagnostic for an owned e-commerce website. It is not a claim about any employer’s results or a universal benchmark for Amazon marketplace conversion. All numbers are illustrative, and the recommended review process is editorial analysis.

Define one conversion measure before comparing it.

For this example, a converted session is an eligible session containing at least one recorded purchase event. Count that session once, even if it contains several purchases. Divide converted sessions by all eligible sessions in the same reporting window. Document exclusions and apply them consistently.

Google defines GA4’s session key event rate using sessions in which a key event occurred divided by total sessions. For a purchase-specific review, select the purchase event rather than treating every configured key event as a sale. Keep session-scoped traffic dimensions consistent with the session-based measure. Source 1 ↗

Transactions per session is a different measure: multiple orders can come from one session. Likewise, a user-based rate has a different denominator. Do not combine those definitions under a single “conversion” label. Check reporting timezone, completed date ranges, event coverage, and any measurement changes before interpreting movement.

Two improving segments can produce a falling total.

Consider two mutually exclusive traffic groups, A and B. These labels describe fictional reporting segments, not actual channels or measured customer intent. Each session belongs to exactly one group in each period.

Group A improves from 10% to 12%, and B from 2% to 3%. But A’s share of sessions falls from one-half to one-quarter. More of the total now comes from the lower-converting group. The combined rate falls by 0.75 percentage points, despite both within-group improvements.

The total is 105 divided by 2,000, not the simple average of 12% and 3%. Averaging those percentages gives 7.5%, which answers a different question: what would the rate be if each group carried equal weight? This reversal is a form of Simpson’s paradox. It is arithmetic, not evidence that the individual rows must be wrong.

Hold the mix constant to make the explanation visible.

Use the first period’s traffic shares as a fixed reference. Apply those 50/50 weights to the second period’s rates: 0.5 × 12% + 0.5 × 3% = 7.5%. Compared with the original 6%, the within-segment rate change contributes +1.5 percentage points under this convention.

The observed second-period rate is 5.25%. The difference between that and the fixed-mix 7.5% is −2.25 percentage points. Together, +1.5 and −2.25 reconcile to the observed −0.75-point movement. This gives the review a precise explanation instead of a vague claim that “traffic quality changed.”

Label the 7.5% as a standardized diagnostic, never as the actual conversion rate. The decomposition depends on the reference weights and order of comparison; another convention can allocate the components differently. It describes the data rather than proving that a campaign caused the changes. New or disappearing segments also require an explicit treatment because a missing historical rate cannot safely be replaced with zero.

Make Power BI recompute the ratio at every level.

Prepare a table named SessionSummary with eligible-session and converted-session counts for disjoint reporting groups. For example, one row can represent a completed day and one session channel. Every eligible session must appear once across the rows being summed. Deduplicate at session level before aggregation; do not sum purchase-event rows as converted sessions.

Purchase session rate =
DIVIDE(
    SUM(SessionSummary[ConvertedSessions]),
    SUM(SessionSummary[EligibleSessions])
)

Format the measure as a percentage. It recomputes the ratio from counts in the current filter context, so totals remain weighted by sessions rather than by the number of displayed rows. Microsoft documents that DIVIDE returns a blank by default when the denominator is zero. Preserve that distinction: no eligible traffic is not an observed 0% conversion rate. Source 2 ↗

Add the two counts and session share beside the percentage. Validate the overall counts against the trusted source before publishing. If you combine channel and device, use mutually exclusive channel-device combinations, not separate channel totals plus separate device totals. Otherwise the same sessions can be counted twice.

Turn the explanation into a better operating decision.

Start the review with three questions: did measurement change, did segment performance change, and did the traffic mix change? Answer them in that order. In the example, both rates improve but the number of converted sessions still falls from 120 to 105. That business outcome remains relevant even after the mix explains the percentage.

  • When the mix shifts: investigate the campaign, audience, device, or availability changes behind the new distribution. Assess acquisition cost and contribution margin before moving budget.
  • When a segment weakens: inspect its landing experience, product availability, delivery promise, and checkout journey. Assign a specific investigation rather than changing every page.
  • When volumes are small: show the counts, examine a longer comparable window, and assess statistical uncertainty before calling a rate difference meaningful. These example improvements are descriptive, not significance claims.

Keep the dashboard compact: actual total rate, converted-session count, segment rates and shares, and one clearly labeled fixed-mix comparison. Record the next action, owner, and review date. A good explanation should narrow the decision, not merely add another chart.

The practical takeaway

Before reacting to a blended conversion rate, inspect the counts and the mix. Better segment performance and a weaker overall result can both be true.
