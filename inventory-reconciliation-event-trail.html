When stock counts disagree, follow the event trail.

A practical inventory reconciliation workflow: align stock definitions, trace movements, investigate discrepancies, and verify the correction across channels.

Compare the same stock at the same moment.

The warehouse count says 96. The inventory system says 100. A marketplace shows 112 available. Changing all three to the smallest number might reduce immediate exposure, but it does not explain what happened. Tomorrow, the same integration could overwrite the correction.

A useful reconciliation connects a quantity to its definition, location, condition, and timestamp. Start by recording those four things before making any adjustment. A physical count of all units cannot be compared directly with a channel quantity that excludes reservations. A morning export cannot establish what was available after an afternoon shipment.

This article proposes an investigation workflow for an inventory discrepancy. It extends a general fulfillment-readiness review into the specific question of how to find and correct the event behind a mismatch. The examples are hypothetical and do not describe employer records or measured business results.

Write down what each quantity means.

Shopify distinguishes on-hand, available, committed, unavailable, and incoming inventory. Its documentation describes on-hand as the total of available, committed, and unavailable units; incoming stock is separate from available stock until received into the appropriate state. [1] Other systems can use different labels and reservation rules, so verify the mapping rather than assuming similarly named fields are equivalent.

For the investigation, choose a physical unit of measure and one location. Separate a single device from a pack, bundle, or accessory kit. Check whether new, refurbished, and damaged units share a product identifier. A valid total across all conditions can still be an unsafe quantity for a new-condition offer.

Record which system owns physical stock and which owns the quantity promised to customers. Ownership should identify who approves an adjustment and which integration may subsequently change it. It does not mean one system is always correct. Physical evidence, transaction history, and timestamps still need to agree.

Reconstruct the movement before changing the balance.

Consider an illustrative SKU at one warehouse. A verified opening count was 120 units. During the review window, the warehouse received 30, shipped 44, and transferred six to another location. Assuming these are the only physical movements, expected closing on-hand stock is 120 + 30 − 44 − 6 = 100 units.

Committed to open orders18Already included in physical on-hand stock
Held for inspection5Also included on hand, but unavailable
Sellable before any additional buffer77100 − 18 − 5

The recount resolves the physical discrepancy without creating an inventory adjustment. The 18 committed units and five inspection units are still physically present, so they must not also be deducted from the on-hand movement calculation. They are deducted when deriving the sellable quantity.

If a channel still displays 112, there is a separate publication or mapping problem. Compare the channel update with the correct sellable basis and its timestamp. Do not use the 35-unit difference as proof of a particular integration fault; it is the size of the exception, not its cause.

An additional buffer would only reduce 77 if it had not already been included in an unavailable or reserved field. Document that treatment explicitly. Counting the same protection twice can unnecessarily suppress sales.

Follow the event trail in both directions.

Trace from the opening balance forward through receipts, shipments, transfers, returns, and corrections. Then work backward from the disputed closing balance. Keep document identifiers beside each movement so another person can reproduce the result without guessing which transaction was included.

Shopify records inventory adjustments with information about when changes occurred, who or what made them, and their effect on quantities. [2] Use the available history in each relevant system to compare the business event with the integration event. A warehouse shipment, an exported message, and a channel acknowledgment are distinct evidence.

Look for missing movements, repeated movements, incorrect units of measure, variant mismatches, and updates delivered out of order. A repeated message should not automatically become a second stock movement. Conversely, two legitimate partial shipments can share an order identifier, so deduplicating every row by order number can erase real activity.

Maintain both the time an event happened and the time it was processed. A late receipt can explain why yesterday’s report changed today. Record corrections as traceable events with reasons instead of silently rewriting the evidence used in the investigation.

Contain the risk, then approve the correction.

Separate the immediate customer risk from the permanent repair. If availability cannot be verified, an authorized owner can temporarily reduce exposure or pause the affected offer under the business’s operating rules. Scope the action to the affected SKU, location, or channel rather than disrupting unrelated products.

Before adjusting stock, recount the relevant bins, inspect receiving and dispatch staging areas, and check returns awaiting disposition. Confirm that no one is moving the same stock during the count, or record every movement during the counting window. A second count performed under the same uncontrolled conditions is weak confirmation.

Approve the correction with a reason, supporting evidence, before-and-after quantity, owner, and timestamp. Then verify that the corrected value reaches downstream channels and remains correct after the next normal update. A temporary manual change is not a durable fix if the integration still derives availability incorrectly.

Measure recurring causes, not only corrected quantities.

A short exception register can capture the SKU, location, comparison cutoff, discrepancy, cause, customer exposure, owner, and resolution evidence. Distinguish confirmed causes from hypotheses. Close an exception only when the records reconcile and the downstream result has been checked.

For reporting, keep stock snapshots separate from movement events. Microsoft’s Power BI modeling guidance emphasizes a consistent grain in fact tables. [3] An event table might represent one inventory movement, while a snapshot table represents one SKU-location-state at a specific time. Summing daily snapshots as if they were receipts would overstate stock.

Review unresolved exception age, repeat incidents by cause, and the share of checked SKU-location records that matched within a defined tolerance. Publish the population and cutoff alongside any accuracy percentage. Checking only stable products can make a broad accuracy claim misleading.

Prioritize problems by customer exposure and recurrence as well as unit count. One missing high-value device or a repeatedly overwritten low-stock offer may deserve attention before a larger discrepancy with an already-understood timing explanation.

Turn one investigation into a repeatable routine.

Start with a small group of products that have recent discrepancies or fast-moving stock. Give warehouse, marketplace, and systems owners the same evidence packet. The review should finish with a specific change to a counting procedure, mapping, integration, or approval rule.

  1. Align SKU, condition, location, units, and cutoff.
  2. Reconstruct physical movements and recount exceptions.
  3. Calculate sellable stock without double deductions.
  4. Trace the publication and acknowledgment history.
  5. Approve the repair and verify the next update.

Revisit the incident after normal activity resumes. Test whether the underlying cause returns during a partial shipment, canceled order, transfer, or return inspection. The strongest outcome is a process that keeps the next quantity credible, with enough evidence to explain it when someone asks.
