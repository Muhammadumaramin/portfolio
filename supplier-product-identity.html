Before a new SKU goes live, prove which product it is.

A wholesale onboarding workflow to verify product identity, resolve pack-size conflicts, and approve accurate Amazon catalog matches before publishing offers.

A supplier row is a starting point, not a listing.

A supplier sends a spreadsheet of available products. One row looks attractive: a familiar brand, a competitive cost, and a product code that returns an Amazon search result. The temptation is to attach an offer, upload stock, and move on. But does that page describe one retail pack, a case of several packs, or a different version of the product?

That question belongs before publication. A convincing title cannot repair a wrong product match. During a busy selling period, the most useful speed improvement may be a clear route for verified products and an equally clear holding area for uncertain ones.

This guide focuses on the handoff from a supplier catalog to an approved marketplace offer. It goes deeper into identity and release controls than a general listing-copy review. The workflow is a recommendation for operators, not an Amazon-mandated process. The example below is entirely hypothetical.

Separate identifiers from evidence.

Keep three identifiers separate in your working file: the supplier’s item code, your internal SKU, and the proposed marketplace product identifier. Each answers a different question. A supplier code locates a purchasing record; an internal SKU organizes your operation; a candidate ASIN points to an Amazon catalog product. A match between text strings does not establish that the physical goods agree.

Amazon explains that product matching lets sellers add offers to existing product pages. Its listing-error guidance says to check the GTIN against the product’s barcode and contact Seller Support when the suggested product is different. Attribute conflicts can also trigger matching errors. [1]

Build an evidence packet containing photographs of the saleable packaging, the printed identifier, brand and model, size or variant, package contents, and the supplier’s dated specification. Preserve the original supplier file. Store identifiers as text so that spreadsheet imports do not discard leading zeros. Keep normalized comparison fields alongside raw values rather than overwriting them.

Verified by GS1 can help check a GTIN’s structure, licensing, and available product attributes. [2] Treat that as one identity check. It does not by itself establish the condition of the units in your warehouse, your supplier’s authorization, or your account’s permission to sell. Investigate a missing or conflicting result; do not treat every incomplete lookup as proof of counterfeit goods.

Define the unit a customer will receive.

Write a plain-language sellable-unit definition before comparing price or quantity. For example: “one sealed retail pack containing two identical cables.” Separately record the purchasing unit: “one outer case containing twelve retail packs.” Those sentences prevent the same word, unit, from carrying incompatible meanings across purchasing, listing, and fulfillment.

Record units per case, items per retail pack, and whether the packaging can be separated for the intended sale. Do not assume a case identifier belongs to the individual retail pack. Inspect the actual packaging level and verify the proposed match against that level. If the supplier offers several variants under a shared description, request variant-specific evidence.

Check the complete candidate page: selected variant, model, quantity, images, and included accessories. A familiar brand and a similar photograph are insufficient. Condition and eligibility also need their own checks before release; identity approval should not silently become approval for every other selling requirement.

Work the pack-size conflict before the upload.

Illustrative example: a wholesaler buys ten cases of a cable product. Each case contains twelve sealed retail packs; each retail pack contains two cables. Assume inspection confirms these contents and the intended offer is for one sealed two-cable pack.

The shipment contains 10 × 12 = 120 retail packs, or 240 individual cables. Neither 10 nor 240 is the intended starting quantity for the two-cable-pack offer. Even 120 is only a physical upper bound: inspection holds, commitments, and your availability rules can reduce the quantity offered.

Suppose the supplier file carries a barcode photographed only on the outer case. That leaves an unresolved identity issue for page B. Ask for the retail-pack identifier and supporting photographs rather than borrowing the candidate page’s identifier to make the upload succeed. If the evidence conflicts with Amazon’s record, resolve the discrepancy through the appropriate support process.

The useful output is an approved mapping with evidence, not merely a successful upload. No sales or return improvement is assumed in this example.

Use three release states with named owners.

A simple intake queue can distinguish ready for release, evidence required, and match rejected. The second state is essential: it lets the team pause an uncertain SKU without confusing it with a permanently unsuitable product. Give each exception an owner, a precise question, and a next review date.

  • Purchasing: obtains supplier clarification and confirms the purchasing unit.
  • Catalog reviewer: compares the physical evidence with the candidate product record and documents the decision.
  • Release owner: checks the approved mapping, selling eligibility, condition, quantity basis, and fulfillment setup before publication.

In a small business, one person may hold multiple roles. A second review is especially useful for bundles, ambiguous pack sizes, changed packaging, or a new supplier. Make the sign-off specific: which supplier item, retail unit, variant, marketplace, and evidence version were approved?

After release, inspect the live offer and an internal pick instruction. Confirm that the customer-facing quantity and warehouse selection describe the same item. A valid catalog match can still fail operationally if a picker interprets “one” as one cable instead of one pack.

Improve throughput without hiding uncertainty.

Start with a small intake batch and record why each SKU waits. Use reason codes such as missing retail identifier, pack-size conflict, variant ambiguity, and eligibility review. Measure time to a documented decision separately from time to publication; an upload delay and an unresolved identity question require different interventions.

Review reopened approvals as well as completed ones. If a supplier repeatedly omits pack definitions, change the intake template and explain the required evidence before the next order. If several reviewers disagree, improve the decision rule using an actual resolved case. Do not reward a high approval count without checking what was approved.

Automation can flag blank identifiers, duplicated mappings, or inconsistent pack arithmetic. It should route uncertain rows for review rather than invent missing values. Store the decision, evidence location, reviewer, and timestamp so the next replenishment can reuse a verified mapping while still checking whether the product has changed.

The practical takeaway

Before publishing an offer, be able to explain what one customer unit contains, why its catalog match is correct, and who approved the evidence. Clear identity makes the rest of the operation easier to trust.
